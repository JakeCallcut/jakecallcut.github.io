An emerging unique visual style has been adopted by some of the biggest names in tech and whether its intentional, reactionary, or accidental, its clear that the trend reflects a shift in visual culture centered around trust and authenticity.

## When Technology Wasn't Cool

Turning back time to the early business PCs from IBM and the classic calculators and watches from Casio in the 1970s, there was an undoubted, function-first design language. With visible ports, grey and beige plastic, its clear that there was no market pressure to make technology desirable. More broadly this reflected an enthusiast culture where rawness and ugliness signified authenticity and proper functionality.

## The Commercial Turn

Perhaps the most iconic product in early consumer technology is the iMac G3. Released in 1998 and designed by Jony Ive, the iMac G3 featured a colourful, egg-shaped, all-in-one case which became the catalyst of personal computing where appearance became the main selling point. Later on, the sequential release of products like the iPod, iPhone, Beats by Dre, and even the Volkswagen Beetle cemented the glossy and colourful aesthetic designed to create desire and inspiration.

## How Tech Became the Villain

Although it is somewhat controversial and difficult to pinpoint, somewhere in the late 2010s this once aspirational sector became viewed with suspicion and restraint. The NSA revelations, where major tech companies were sharing data with government surveillance programs, marked a serious breach of public trust. This, along with other scandals involving poor labour conditions and electoral manipulation prompted serious regulatory response with legislation such as GDPR and CCPA signed into law by the EU and California respectively in 2018.

Lately, the inception of mainstream generative AI gave a lot of people a genuine fear about job displacement, and massively upset the creative industry of writers, artists, and actors. After most people had accepted that their data was being used and misused, this became more about the outsourcing and extraction of intellectual and creative work.  

A cultural shift toward 'switching off' and not interacting with technology is in play, fuelled by widespread concern about things like the AI, environmental impact of data centres, or the marriage of defence and technology. This forms the final and current wave of technology skepticism, where environmental impacts/water usage are no longer covert, and military tech contracts are openly discussed and marketed. All of this has built a villainous image for the tech industry which was once an exciting sector built on design and aspiration.  


## The Industry Response

Broadsheet style, warm-beige, serif fonts, 35mm film advertisements. All techniques used in marketing material and software by a plethora of AI-adjacent companies like Anthropic, Apple, Granola, Harvey, and OpenAI. This backward shift in design language has been documented in most areas of modern life, but the direct communication from tech companies is a clear message that rough, vintage style is a mark of authenticity. 

### Case Study: The Palantir Chore Coat

on 30th April 2026, as part of their new 'tradecraft' collection, American software powerhouse *Palantir* released the 'Lightweight Chore Coat' in blue and black, which sold out quickly. The jacket features the brand's logo on the breast pocket and even an enscription of a quote from the CTO on the inner lining.

<center><img src="/images/chore-coat.png" alt="Inner patch of the Palantir Chore Coat" width="800"/></center>

The release of this jacket, and a Palantir clothing and accessory line in general marks a significant change in brand communication. A government contracted software company releasing fashion products would have been unthinkable before.

The irony of a company with upwards of a $300b market capitalisation releasing clothes with a history of artisinal French labour and the European creative class is perhaps intentional, but does come across as an imitation and playing into an all-american industrial falsehood. Either way, the message is clear, big industry is developing a tasteful style which is hard to ignore.

### Case Study: The Apple TV Intro

A more positive example of dedication to craft and process is the new(ish) Apple TV intro. First introduced in November of 2025, the 5 second clip with 16 terabytes of raw footage and rich audio behind it, contains zero CGI or 3D rendering.

<center><img src="/images/apple-tv-intro.png" alt="Freezeframe of the Apple TV intro" width="800"/></center>

The team at Apple, in collaboration with TBWA\Media Arts Lab and Optical Arts hand crafted, real translucent glass apples and used a manual rotation of the sculpture to acheive the shimmering cinematics.

Alongside the short clip, Apple released an official behind the scenes video showing the audio and visual work. Displaying a knowing awareness of the companies own tasteful implementation.

Both cases point to the same underlying fact: taste has become load-bearing in a way it wasn't a decade ago. What's harder to answer is whether that's a sign of an industry maturing, learning to communicate honestly after years of opacity, or an industry that has simply found a new material to manufacture trust from, cheaper and faster than earning it the old way.
